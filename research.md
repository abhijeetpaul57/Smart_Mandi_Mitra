# Step 1: Find and Research — MandiMitra

## 1. Problem Statement (5 Lines)
1. Local vegetable markets (mandis) feature dense, linear, or labyrinthine layouts where vendors are distributed across entrance lanes, middle lanes, and deep inner lanes.
2. Casual shoppers predominantly purchase their daily produce from the first few vendors encountered near the market entrance gates to minimize walking and carrying loads.
3. As a result, inner-lane vendors experience significantly lower foot traffic and fewer buyer interactions despite often carrying fresh produce at equal or lower prices.
4. Existing quick-commerce and hyper-local delivery apps bypass street vendors entirely, while informal physical markets lack any centralized informational infrastructure.
5. This project investigates whether a simple entrance directory and transparent vendor discovery system can eliminate information asymmetry and increase customer awareness for inner-lane vendors.

## 3. Two Existing Solutions & Why They Do Not Fit This Person

### Solution 1: Quick-Commerce Apps (Blinkit / Zepto / Instamart)
- **What it does**: Delivers groceries to urban consumers within 10–15 minutes from dark warehouses.
- **Why it does not fit this person**:
  - Dark-store aggregators procure inventory from industrial agricultural supply chains and directly compete against street vendors rather than onboarding them.
  - Informal street vendors lack smartphones with merchant apps, GST registration, bank POS machines, and margins to pay 15–25% platform commissions.

### Solution 2: Digital Wholesale Portals (e-NAM / Agmarknet)
- **What it does**: Government electronic trading portals for APMC mandis to facilitate agricultural wholesale trading between farmers and licensed commission agents.
- **Why it does not fit this person**:
  - Targets bulk transactions (quintals/truckloads) between farmers and wholesale traders, not end-consumer retail sales.
  - Completely unusable for an individual micro-retailer sitting on a street mat selling 15 kg of produce per day to pedestrian shoppers.

## 4. Two Research Sources with Reading Notes

### Source 1: Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act & Urban Informal Economy Studies
- **Source Citation / Reference**: Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs (MoHUA), Government of India — Periodic Labour Force Surveys (PLFS) & Urban Informal Market Studies on Spatial Disadvantage of Non-Motorized Vendors.
- **My Reading Notes**:
  - Note Line 1: Street vending natural markets exhibit steep footfall drop-offs; vendors located more than 30–40 meters from transit nodes capture less than 30% of entry volume.
  - Note Line 2: Ground-level women vendors lacking permanent storefront structures face the highest spatial exclusion, forcing them to sell perishable greens at clearance rates near market close.
  - Note Line 3: Non-monetary informational interventions (e.g. entrance boards and transparent zoning) have shown positive effects in redistributing pedestrian density in dense bazars.

### Source 2: Consumer Behavior in Fresh Food Retail & Proximity Bias
- **Source Citation / Reference**: Empirical studies on consumer path choice and retail density in traditional wet markets (e.g., Food Policy / Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services on physical search cost in open-air markets).
- **My Reading Notes**:
  - Note Line 1: Shoppers purchasing heavy, bulky produce (potatoes, onions, tomatoes) experience high physical carrying friction and prioritize stopping at the earliest stall.
  - Note Line 2: Price dispersion exists across traditional open markets, with inner stalls discounting produce by 10–25% to attract buyers, but consumers fail to discover this without upfront pricing cues.
  - Note Line 3: An entrance information board functions as a low-cost cognitive anchor, lowering perceived search friction and encouraging shoppers to walk deeper into the market.

## 5. Questions to Verify During Fieldwork
1. Does the vendor feel that their stall position deeper inside the lane reduces their daily customer count compared to stalls at the entrance?
2. Do inner vendors deliberately lower their prices to compensate for lower foot traffic, or do fixed wholesale procurement costs prevent price cutting?
3. How much unsold produce remains at the end of the evening, and does spoilage force distress selling at a loss?
4. How do daily customers decide whether to walk to the back of the market or stop at the first entrance stall?
5. Would an entrance information board displaying stall numbers, stock, and prices genuinely motivate customers to walk deeper inside?
